About Me

I'm interested in how physical networks transport resources, respond to forces, and learn. These systems range from disordered network materials to biological networks. This includes both the dynamics on networks and the dynamics of the networks themselves. My research is driven by a desire to understand how local interactions and rules can produce unexpected collective behavior, and how that behavior depends on network structure. I study these questions using ideas from complex systems and statistical physics, with my current work focused on network metamaterials and physical learning.

I’m also interested in using network models and statistical physics to understand the dynamics and adaptation of neuronal networks and large-scale brain networks.

Current Research

How network structure shapes physical learning

Suppose we want a spring network to move very little when driven over a chosen range of frequencies. Its response depends on the stiffness and arrangement of springs throughout the network. Yet the motion at the two ends of a single spring also depends on that surrounding structure. Can we use these local measurements to guide changes in individual springs until the network has the response we want?

I study this question by training spring networks to open a spectral gap, an interval with no vibrational modes. The rule I developed adjusts each spring’s stiffness using the relative motion of its ends under two related patterns of applied forces. As those stiffnesses change, the network responds differently to the next drive. Repeated local adjustments can move its vibrational frequencies out of the chosen interval, leaving a gap in the spectrum.

A trained spring network driven by a random force pattern as the driving frequency increases. Its displacement is small inside the spectral gap. Edges are coloured by their trained stiffness, darker for stiffer. Right: the trained eigenfrequencies (top) and the network’s response amplitude as a function of driving frequency (bottom).

The same rule does not work equally well on every network. Some networks develop a gap readily, while others retain modes in the chosen interval. I compare network structures and frequency windows to understand how the arrangement of connections shapes what a network can learn through local changes. This is joint work with Pola Pietrzkowski, Linnéa Gyllingberg, Menachem Stern, and Mason Porter.

In the displayed runs, Delaunay and Watts–Strogatz networks have no eigenfrequencies left in the window; Barabási–Albert and Erdős–Rényi networks retain some. Transport and stiffness in scale-rich metamaterials

A material made from a network of beams can be easy to compress in one direction and much harder to compress in another. Each beam resists deformation, but the response of the material also depends on how the beams connect. I study this in scale-rich metamaterials (Both et al., 2025), where beams of very different lengths and thicknesses meet in an irregular network. I want to understand which features of this arrangement make some directions stiffer than others.

One way I approach this question is to ask how current would flow through the same structure. I represent each beam segment as a conducting edge and each junction as a node, then compare electrical transport through this network with the material’s response to compression. Across samples cut at different orientations, larger effective conductance tends to accompany larger compressive stiffness in finite-element simulations. This comparison helps identify the directions in which the material is stiffer, although it does not give the magnitude of the stiffness. I work on this with Charles Emmett Maher, Katherine Newhall, and Mason Porter.

Directional effective resistance in scale-rich networks. Left: an illustrative network with α = 1. Middle: resistance between pairs of boundary nodes in six opposing directions on a circular sample, for a separate realization with α = 1. The largest and smallest sampled values differ by a factor of about 15. Right: the mean ratio of the largest to the smallest sampled resistance over 5,000 realizations at each α. Larger α produces greater differences in beam thickness and, in this ensemble, stronger directional variation in resistance.

Connecting topology and statistical physics

Imagine joining nearby points in a pattern, then gradually allowing longer connections. Small clusters form and merge into larger ones. A single new connection might join just two points, or bring together two large clusters. Both events reduce the number of clusters by one, but the second connects many more pairs of points. How can we keep track of these differences as the clusters grow?

Persistent homology, a tool from topological data analysis, records the distances at which clusters merge. Its standard summary leaves out the cluster sizes. I developed a weighted persistence method that also counts how many pairs of points become connected for the first time at each merger. From this record, I can reconstruct the mean size of the cluster containing a point chosen uniformly at random, at every connection distance. The weighted record also distinguishes point patterns with closely matched pair correlation functions and structure factors. I am writing this work up with Charles Emmett Maher, Katherine Newhall, and Mason Porter.

A point pattern and its growing simplicial complex. Delaunay edges enter when their endpoints are within 2ε, and triangles enter when all three edges are present. Triangles are shaded by their entry scale, darker for earlier, and enclosed voids are coloured by area. Right: each cluster merger weighted by the product of the two cluster sizes (top), and the mean cluster size seen by a randomly chosen point (bottom). The curve reconstructed from the weighted mergers agrees with direct counts of cluster sizes (dots).

Disordered networks from hyperuniform point patterns

A pattern of points can look irregular up close and still be evenly spread out over larger regions. Imagine moving a large window across the pattern and counting how many points fall inside it. In a disordered hyperuniform pattern, this count varies less from place to place than it would for points scattered independently at random, as in a Poisson pattern. This form of spatial order has been found in the centers of loops in leaf vein networks and in simulated galaxy distributions.

How does this spatial order affect transport once we use the points to construct a network? The answer also depends on how we construct the connections. In my work with collaborators, I compare four network constructions using total effective resistance, which combines effective resistances between all pairs of nodes. For three of the four constructions, networks generated from hyperuniform patterns have a lower mean normalized total effective resistance than their Poisson counterparts. To understand why some connections matter much more than others, I also examine how the geometry around an edge relates to the increase in total effective resistance when that edge is removed.

These networks also provide designs for physical materials. Through an NSF-funded collaboration (DMREF), I chose the point patterns and construction methods for networks that Christopher Rock’s group at NC State 3D-prints and Karen Daniels’s group measures. As one of the theorists in this collaboration, I study how the materials’ stiffness per unit mass changes as disorder increases.

Poisson (top) and hyperuniform (bottom) point patterns, their Delaunay networks, and their structure factors S(k). Hyperuniform patterns suppress long-wavelength density fluctuations, so S(k) → 0 as k → 0.

Publications

Local Geometric and Transport Properties of Networks that are Generated from Hyperuniform Point Patterns
J. V. Raj, X. Sun, C. E. Maher, K. A. Newhall, M. A. Porter. arXiv:2511.21082 (2025); under review at Physical Review E.
Abstract ▼
Hyperuniformity, which is a type of order that is characterized by the suppression of long-range density fluctuations in comparison to the fluctuations in standard disordered systems, has emerged as a powerful concept in many natural and engineered phenomena. In this paper, we harness hyperuniform point patterns to generate disordered, spatially-embedded networks that are distinct from both perfectly ordered lattices and uniformly random spatial networks. We refer to this class of disordered networks as hyperuniform-point-pattern-induced (HuPPI) networks, and we compare them to counterpart Poisson-point-pattern-induced (PoPPI) networks and networks that we generate from random-sequential-adsorption (RSA) packings (which have local order but are not hyperuniform). We examine HuPPI, PoPPI, and RSA networks that we generate using Gabriel, Delaunay, Delaunay-centroidal, and Voronoi network-generation methods. We show that HuPPI networks have smaller mean normalized total effective resistances (TERs) than PoPPI networks in Gabriel, Delaunay-centroidal, and Voronoi networks and have shorter mean random-walk mixing times than PoPPI networks in Gabriel, Delaunay, and Delaunay-centroidal networks. For all four network-generation methods and for both HuPPI and PoPPI networks, we find that the Ollivier–Ricci curvature (ORC) of an edge is negatively correlated with its TER edge contribution (i.e., the increase in TER that results from removing that edge). Additionally, PoPPI networks have wider distributions of TER edge contributions than their counterpart HuPPI networks. The edges in PoPPI networks with the largest TER edge contributions have negative ORCs and act as critical bridges; removing them severely disrupts global transport. The scarcity of such bridging edges in HuPPI networks helps explain why they have smaller mean normalized TERs than their PoPPI counterparts in Gabriel, Delaunay-centroidal, and Voronoi networks, including the counterintuitive observation that Delaunay-centroidal and Voronoi HuPPI networks have smaller mean normalized TERs despite having more-negative mean ORCs. Our comparison of HuPPI networks and PoPPI networks with RSA networks reveals some similarities between HuPPI and RSA networks in mean normalized TER and mean ORC, and it demonstrates that local order impacts the observed differences between HuPPI and PoPPI networks. For all four network-generation methods, both HuPPI and PoPPI networks are less robust (i.e., the size of their largest connected component decays more rapidly) when we remove edges in the order of their ORC values, starting with the most-negative ORCs, than when we remove edges uniformly at random. In Gabriel networks, the HuPPI networks are more robust than their PoPPI counterparts for both edge-removal strategies. Collectively, our results (1) illustrate how the local network geometry that ORC measures is related to transport and network connectivity and (2) demonstrate that the network-generation method provides an additional design lever for spatial networks beyond the choice of their underlying point pattern.
Phase-field simulations of morphology development in reactive polymer blending
R. Sengupta, M. D. Tikekar, J. V. Raj, K. T. Delaney, M. C. Villet, G. H. Fredrickson. Journal of Rheology 67(1), 1-14 (2023).
Abstract ▼
Reactive blending is an efficient method for synthesizing polymer blends. Industrially, this process is carried out in extruders where reacting polymers and generated copolymers are subjected to high shear stresses. The dynamics and resulting morphology are dictated by coupling of hydrodynamic forces in the extruder, thermodynamic interactions between species, and reaction kinetics on a complex interfacial manifold. We use phase-field simulations to quantify the evolution of reactive blending under external shear flow for model systems of two homopolymers of equal length that react via end-coupling to produce a diblock copolymer. We compare morphology development in cylindrical thread and droplet geometries. Our results indicate that shear suppresses Rayleigh capillary instability in threads while accelerating reaction rates in drops by expanding the interfacial contact area.
