About Me

I am a fifth-year physics PhD student at UCLA, advised by Mason Porter. My research is in complex systems and statistical physics, and most of it concerns networks. I am most interested in physical learning, in which networks of springs or resistors learn a task through local rules (each edge adjusts itself using only what it can measure), and in how the structure of a network limits what such rules can learn and how quickly.

I am also interested in living networks that adapt by similar local rules, such as slime molds and blood vessels that reorganize around the flow that they carry, and synapses that change with neural activity. Alongside physical learning, I work on topological data analysis and its connections to the statistical physics of clustering, and on spatial networks, including disordered metamaterials that our experimental collaborators 3D-print.

Before UCLA, I studied physics at Cal Poly, San Luis Obispo, where I worked on memory in sheared suspensions with Hilary Jacks and Nathan Keim, and I spent a year in Glenn Fredrickson's group at UC Santa Barbara simulating reactive polymer blending. I expect to finish my PhD in June 2027 and am looking for postdoctoral positions.

Current Research

Network structure shapes accessible solutions in physical learning

To compare structures fairly, we use a task that is defined on the whole network, with no source or target nodes to choose: clearing a window of vibrational frequencies in a disordered spring network, so that none of its eigenfrequencies lies inside the window. In our local rule, random forces at frequencies inside the window drive the network, and each spring changes its stiffness using only the motion of its own two nodes. No step computes an eigenfrequency, so the same rule can train materials far too large for centralized design.

Whether the rule succeeds, and how quickly, depends strongly on the structure of the network. Delaunay and Watts-Strogatz networks typically clear the window, whereas many runs on Erdős-Rényi networks never do. Even rewiring a Watts-Strogatz network so that every node keeps its number of edges more than doubles the time that it takes to learn.

Eigenfrequency distributions before and after training for one representative run on each of four network families, with the target window shaded. Beside each histogram is the corresponding trained network, with each edge coloured by its learned weight.

Understanding Network Properties of Scale-Rich Metamaterials

In the scale-rich metamaterials of Both et al. (2025), ligament thicknesses span orders of magnitude. With Charles Emmett Maher, Katherine Newhall, and Mason Porter, I model these materials as spatially embedded networks of beams that can be printed directly. A graph conductance on the network predicts how the stiffness depends on the direction of loading (though not its magnitude), without a mechanical simulation for each direction.

Directional effective resistance Reff becomes more anisotropic as the heterogeneity parameter α grows. Left: an illustrative scale-rich network with α = 1. Middle: Reff along different directions for one realization with α = 1, whose largest and smallest values differ by a factor of about 15. Right: the ratio of the largest to the smallest Reff, averaged over 5,000 realizations for each α.

TDA for Clustering Detection

Persistent homology, a tool from topological data analysis, records the scales at which clusters of points merge, but the observables of statistical physics depend on the sizes of the merging clusters. Charles Emmett Maher, Katherine Newhall, Mason Porter, and I are developing a size-resolved persistent homology that records these sizes, which links topological data analysis directly to the statistical physics of clustering.

A clustered pattern of 92 points and its Vietoris-Rips simplicial complex at three values of the filtration radius ε (points within 2ε of each other are joined). As ε grows, the six clusters connect into a loop, which then fills in. Persistent homology tracks when such features appear and disappear.

Disordered networks from hyperuniform point patterns

Hyperuniform point patterns are disordered but suppress density fluctuations at large length scales, and they appear in settings from leaf vein networks to the large-scale distribution of galaxies. With Xiaohan Sun, Charles Emmett Maher, Katherine Newhall, and Mason Porter, I showed that, for three of the four ways of building networks that we compared, networks from hyperuniform patterns have smaller total effective resistances than networks from Poisson patterns. In an NSF DMREF collaboration, the groups of Karen Daniels and Christopher Rock at NC State 3D-print networks that I design, and we are working out why their specific modulus depends on how a network is built.

Poisson (top) and hyperuniform (bottom) point patterns, their Delaunay networks, and their structure factors S(k). Hyperuniform patterns suppress long-wavelength density fluctuations, so S(k) → 0 as k → 0.

Publications

Local Geometric and Transport Properties of Networks that are Generated from Hyperuniform Point Patterns
J. V. Raj, X. Sun, C. E. Maher, K. A. Newhall, M. A. Porter. arXiv:2511.21082 (2025); resubmitted to Physical Review E.
Abstract ▼
Hyperuniformity, which is a type of long-range order that is characterized by the suppression of long-range density fluctuations in comparison to the fluctuations in standard disordered systems, has emerged as a powerful concept to aid in the understanding of diverse natural and engineered phenomena. In the present paper, we harness hyperuniform point patterns to generate a class of disordered, spatially embedded networks that are distinct from both perfectly ordered lattices and uniformly random geometric graphs. We refer to these networks as hyperuniform-point-pattern-induced (HuPPI) networks, and we compare them to their counterpart Poisson-point-pattern-induced (PoPPI) networks. By computing the local geometric and transport properties of HuPPI networks, we demonstrate how hyperuniformity imparts advantages in both transport efficiency and robustness. Specifically, we show that HuPPI networks have systematically smaller total effective resistances, slightly faster random-walk mixing times, and fewer extreme-curvature edges than PoPPI networks. Counterintuitively, we also find that HuPPI networks simultaneously have more negative mean Ollivier–Ricci curvatures and smaller total effective resistances than PoPPI networks, indicating that edges with moderately negative curvatures need not create severe bottlenecks to transport. Moreover, HuPPI networks are consistently more robust under both random edge removals and curvature-based targeted edge removals, maintaining larger connected components for larger fractions of removed edges than their PoPPI counterparts. We also demonstrate that the network-generation method strongly influences these properties and in particular that it often overshadows differences that arise from underlying point patterns.
Phase-field simulations of morphology development in reactive polymer blending
R. Sengupta, M. D. Tikekar, J. V. Raj, K. T. Delaney, M. C. Villet, G. H. Fredrickson. Journal of Rheology 67(1), 1-14 (2023).
Abstract ▼
Reactive blending is an efficient method for synthesizing polymer blends. Industrially, this process is carried out in extruders where reacting polymers and generated copolymers are subjected to high shear stresses. The dynamics and resulting morphology are dictated by coupling of hydrodynamic forces in the extruder, thermodynamic interactions between species, and reaction kinetics on a complex interfacial manifold. We use phase-field simulations to quantify the evolution of reactive blending under external shear flow for model systems of two homopolymers of equal length that react via end-coupling to produce a diblock copolymer. We compare morphology development in cylindrical thread and droplet geometries. Our results indicate that shear suppresses Rayleigh capillary instability in threads while accelerating reaction rates in drops by expanding the interfacial contact area.
